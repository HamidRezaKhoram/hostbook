# The Hostbook Guide: How to Host Gatherings People Remember

This is a practical field guide. You don't need to be a natural host. You need a reason, a plan, and a few habits that do most of the work. Everything here is something you can act on. Where a claim comes from research or a named expert, the source is in [`../research/research-notes.md`](../research/research-notes.md).

> **The one-sentence version:** Know why you're gathering, invite the right people, tell them exactly what you need from them, set up the room so they mix, open warmly, end on purpose, and say thank you.

## 1. Start with the why

Priya Parker (*The Art of Gathering*) warns about a common mistake: treating the *category* of the event as its purpose. "A birthday party" or "a dinner" is a category. A purpose is what you want to be different because people gathered.

**Write one sentence before you plan anything else:**

- ❌ "Saturday dinner."
- ✅ "Get my work friends and my climbing friends into one room so they stop being two separate worlds."
- ✅ "Welcome Sam back from six months away and let them tell their stories."
- ✅ "A no-phones cottage weekend where everyone actually rests."

This sentence settles later decisions for you: who to invite (and who not to), how big it is, whether there's a toast, whether there are games, how long it runs.

**Give it a name.** "Dumpling Night", "The Leaf-Peeping Weekend", "Sam's Homecoming." Parker suggests naming the gathering, and a name tells guests what kind of night it will be before they arrive.

**Don't shrink a people problem into a logistics problem.** Parker's warning: when we focus on the stuff (napkins, playlists), we "shrink a human challenge down to a logistical one." Logistics matter, and this app exists for them, but they serve the purpose. They don't replace it.

## 2. Pick the format

| Format | Size | Length | Host effort | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dinner party (seated) | 4–10 | 3–4 h | High | Deep conversation, celebrating someone |
| Potluck | 6–30 | 3–4 h | Low–medium | Big groups, low budget, "everyone contributes" |
| Cocktail / drinks party | 10–40 | **2 h** | Medium | Mixing friend groups, meeting new people |
| Open house / drop-in | 15–60 | 4–6 h window | Medium | Holidays, housewarmings, mixed ages |
| BBQ / backyard | 8–40 | 3–5 h | Medium | Summer, families, casual |
| Game night | 4–12 | 3–4 h | Low | Friends who already know each other |
| Movie night | 3–12 | 3–3.5 h | Low | Cozy nights, low-key friends, rainy weekends |
| Theme party | 10–40 | 3–5 h | High | Big energy, dressing up |
| Cottage / weekend trip | 4–16 | 2–3 days | Very high (share it) | Real bonding; needs meal rota and roles |

Rules of thumb:
- **Smaller is easier to make great.** Under about 8 people you can have one shared conversation. Over that, the party splits into small groups (see §9), and your job turns into mixing people.
- **Shorter beats longer.** Nick Gray's *2-Hour Cocktail Party* format works because it's short. People stay engaged and leave wanting more.
- **Match effort to energy.** If you're tired this month, hold a potluck, not a five-course dinner. A relaxed host makes for relaxed guests.

## 3. The guest list

**Chemistry first, then count.**
- Start with 1–2 "anchor" guests the gathering is really for.
- Add **connectors**, people who talk to anyone and introduce others. One connector per 6–8 guests helps a lot.
- Mix, but on purpose. Two or three friend groups that overlap a little works well. One lone stranger among a tight group often goes badly; if you invite a new person, invite them with someone, or brief a connector to look after them.
- **Be willing to leave people out.** Parker lists this as necessary. Inviting someone who works against the purpose (the coworker at the "no work talk" dinner) hurts everyone else's night.

**Plus-ones and kids.** Decide this and *say it in the invitation*. Ambiguity here causes the most awkward follow-ups.

**Plan for people not showing.**
- For loose, free RSVPs, the most solid data we found shows a median no-show rate of about 28% (37% for small events of 10–49 people). Those numbers come from public events.
- With friends and a personal invite, expect much less. **Our working estimate: 10–20% of "yes" and about 50% of "maybe" won't come.** That's a heuristic, not a measured number.
- So: invite a few more than the room needs, but **cook for confirmed yeses plus 10–15%**.

**Record dietary needs and accessibility when you invite**, not on the day (see §8).

## 4. Date, time, and the end time

- **Check the anchor guests' availability first**, then announce.
- **Weeknights are underrated.** Nick Gray recommends Monday–Wednesday for 2-hour parties: calendars are emptier and people come because the bar to say yes is low.
- **Put an end time on the invitation.** "7–9 pm" or "7 until late" are both fine; leaving it unstated is the problem. An end time lets people plan, makes the night feel lively, and gives you a clean way to close (§12).
- Avoid starting dinner parties later than people's normal dinner time. Hungry guests are unhappy guests.

## 5. Invitations and reminders

**Lead time**

| Event | Send invites |
|---|---|
| Casual drinks / game night | 1–2 weeks |
| Dinner party | 2–3 weeks |
| Holidays (Dec), big birthdays | 4–6 weeks |
| Cottage weekend / trip | 6–10 weeks (people need time off and rides) |

**Every invitation answers:**
1. **What and why**: the name and one line of purpose.
2. **When**: start *and* end, plus a different arrival time for anyone helping.
3. **Where**: address, map link, parking, buzzer or door code, which door.
4. **Who**: plus-ones yes/no, kids yes/no, roughly how many people.
5. **What to bring**: specific (§6), or "nothing but yourself."
6. **Food**: full meal, snacks, or eat first. Guests hate guessing this.
7. **Dress / vibe**: "Jeans are fine", "Outside by the fire, bring a warm layer."
8. **How to reply, and by when.**
9. **Dietary needs**: ask now.

**Reminders.** Partiful hosts report that a few short reminders noticeably improve turnout. A good cadence:
- **~1 week before:** "Still on! Here's what you're bringing: ___."
- **Day before / morning of:** logistics only: time, address, parking, door code, weather.
- **During (optional):** "We're out back, door's open."

Keep reminders short. Send logistics to the people who are coming, not the whole invite list.

## 6. Asking people to bring things and to help

This is the core of what Hostbook does.

**People want to contribute, but they want a clear job.** "Bring whatever" gives you four bags of chips. "Can you bring a green salad for 10?" gets a green salad for 10.

**How to ask well:**
- **Be specific: item, amount, and serving needs.** "A dessert for 12, cut-ready, bring a serving knife."
- **Ask, then confirm.** An assignment isn't settled until they say yes.
- **Match the job to the person.** The friend who loves to cook gets the main side. The friend who doesn't cook gets ice and drinks. The friend coming straight from work gets something to pick up at a store on the way, not something to make.
- **Use categories for big potlucks**, with more detail inside each: *Mains · Sides · Salads · Desserts · Drinks · Ice · Bread · Paper goods*. Specific slots ("pasta salad", "fruit salad") avoid duplicates better than one open "salad" spot.
- **Make the boring things their own slots**: ice, cups, napkins, serving spoons, trash bags, a cooler, extension cord, folding chairs. They're the first to be forgotten.
- **Ask dish-bringers to label** their dish with main ingredients, so people with allergies can choose safely.
- **Don't outsource the anchor.** The host covers the main thing the party depends on (the main dish at a dinner, the grill at a BBQ). If a guest's contribution falls through, the party should still work.

**Helping jobs (not just bringing):**

| Job | When | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Setup crew (chairs, tables, lights) | 60–90 min early | Practical friends |
| Door / greeter, coats | First hour | Warm, outgoing friends |
| Bar / drinks restocker | All night | Someone who likes to move around |
| Grill master / kitchen help | Meal time | Confident cooks |
| Music | All night | The friend with opinions |
| Photographer | Highlights | Anyone, just ask |
| Toast / game lead | One moment | Funny or sentimental friends |
| Cleanup crew | Last 30 min + after | Close friends staying late |
| Driver / designated sober person | Whole night | Someone who offers |

People feel more invested in a party they helped make. Small jobs give shy guests something to do with their hands.

## 7. Arrival times: who shows up when

**The empty room problem.** The first guests walk into a quiet, bright, half-ready house and feel awkward. Fix this by **staggering arrivals on purpose**:

| Who | Arrive | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Setup helpers | Start − 60 to 90 min | Furniture, ice, last errands |
| Close friends / "seeds" | Start − 15 to 30 min | So the first real guest walks into people already talking |
| Main guests | Start | Typically arrive over 30–45 minutes |
| Guests from far / with kids | Flexible | Tell them it's fine |
| Cleanup crew | Stay after end | Ask in advance, don't assume |

**Tell each person their own time.** Don't send helpers the main time and hope. Each guest should get one message with *their* arrival time and *their* job. That's what Hostbook's per-guest links are for.

**For dinner parties**, decide "arrive by" and "we eat at." A 30–45 minute buffer between them lets late people arrive without ruining the food.

## 8. Food and drink

Full numbers: **[quantities.md](quantities.md).** Quick version:

- **Drinks:** about 2 per guest in the first hour and 1 per hour after. A 4-hour party is about 5 per person.
- **Ice:** 0.5–0.7 kg (1–1.5 lb) per guest for drinks, double if it's hot or outdoors, plus more for chilling coolers.
- **Food:** a full meal is about 450–700 g (1–1.5 lb) of total food per person. Add 10–15% for buffets. Running out is worse than leftovers.
- **Always offer a good non-alcoholic option**, not just water and a flat soda. Think spritzers, a mocktail, nice sparkling water, NA beer. Put it next to the alcohol so choosing it isn't conspicuous.
- **Water station** in a visible place, easy to refill.

**Dietary needs**
- **Health Canada's priority allergens:** peanuts, tree nuts, sesame, milk, eggs, fish, crustaceans and molluscs (shellfish), soy, wheat and triticale, mustard, and sulphites, plus gluten sources (wheat, barley, rye, oats). The US list of 8 (peanuts, tree nuts, milk, eggs, wheat, soy, fish, shellfish) causes about 90% of food allergies.
- Ask in three buckets: **allergies/intolerances** (safety, top priority) · **diets** (vegetarian, vegan, gluten-free) · **religious** (halal, kosher, no pork, no alcohol).
- Make sure every guest has at least one substantial thing they can eat, not just salad.
- Keep allergens physically apart, use separate serving spoons, and label dishes.

**Make-ahead is your friend.** Pick a menu where at most one thing needs last-minute attention. You should spend the party with your guests, not your stove.

## 9. Setting the space

**Conversation is small-group.** Research from Robin Dunbar's group finds spontaneous conversations top out around **4 people**. In observed social groups the average conversation size was about 2.75, and no group larger than 6 stayed one single conversation. So:
- Create **several small conversation spots** (2–5 seats close together), not one big ring of chairs around the walls.
- Face-to-face beats side-by-side. One study found people only chose side-by-side seating when face-to-face at under about 5.5 ft wasn't possible. Couches are bad for talking; angle chairs toward each other.
- For seated dinners over 8, expect the table to split into 2–3 conversations. Seat people on purpose: separate couples, put connectors in the middle, put the shy guest next to the kind talker.

**Flow**
- **Separate food and drinks**, ideally on opposite sides of the room. This spreads people out and stops a jam in one spot. Keep both away from the front door so arrivals don't hit a wall of people.
- A **slightly too-small room is better than a too-big one.** A full room feels lively, a half-empty room feels like a failure. For standing parties, an often-quoted planner rule is **about 1 m² (10 sq ft) per person**. Close doors to rooms you don't want used.
- **Coats:** one clear place (a bed, a rack). Tell guests where as they arrive.
- **Bathroom:** stocked (toilet paper, hand towel, soap), unlocked, a light on, obvious. Add a plunger, discreetly.
- **Shoes:** if it's a shoes-off home, say so in the invite and offer a spot or slippers.
- **Pets:** tell guests in advance (allergies, fears). Give the pet a quiet room.

**Lighting**
- **Turn off the overhead lights.** Use lamps, string lights, candles: warm, low, at different heights. Make pools of light rather than lighting everything evenly. Warm bulbs and dimmers help.
- Exceptions: keep the kitchen, food table, stairs, and path to the bathroom bright enough to be safe.

**Music**
- **Prepare more music than you think you'll need.** One guide suggests about 3 hours (~75 songs) queued, another suggests twice the party length.
- **Volume: if people are leaning in to hear each other, it's too loud.** Turn it down as conversation picks up, up as the room loosens.
- Start mellow while people arrive, build energy in the middle, wind down at the end (§12).
- Check neighbors and quiet hours. A heads-up note to neighbors buys a lot of goodwill.

**Smell and temperature.** A room with 20 people warms up fast. Start a bit cool, crack a window.

## 10. The first 30 minutes

Parker calls **the arrival and the opening** the two moments that matter most. People decide early whether this is a good party.

**Be at the door.** The host should be ready (dressed, food out, music on) **before** the first guest arrives. Do your last-minute tasks 15 minutes before, not after.

**The 5-minutes-before check:** lights set · music on · candles lit · drinks out with ice · one appetizer out · bathroom ready · you have a drink in your hand.

**Welcome every person:**
1. Greet by name, warmly, and look happy to see them.
2. Take their coat and their dish, or tell them where things go.
3. **Get a drink into their hand within 2 minutes.**
4. **Introduce them to someone with a hook**, more than a name. "This is Priya, she just got back from Japan. Priya, Leo is planning a trip there in spring." Say how each person knows you and one thing they share.
5. Then let go. Don't stay stuck by their side.

**Name tags** sound corny and work, especially for 15+ people with several friend groups. Nick Gray insists they be non-optional. Write first names large, by hand.

**Icebreakers**, if you use them, should be easy and low-stakes at first. Save deeper questions for later, once the room has warmed up. Examples:
- "What's the best thing that happened to you this month?"
- "What's something you're looking forward to?"
- At a dinner: one shared question around the table between mains and dessert.

**Mark the start.** A short toast or welcome once most people are there ("Thanks for coming, here's why we're here...") makes a hangout into an occasion. 60 seconds is enough.

## 11. Running the middle

The host's job during the party is mostly **noticing**:
- **Scan the room every 15–20 minutes.** Who's alone? Which group is stuck? Who's had no food?
- **Rescue alone guests** by bringing them into a group with an introduction, or giving them a small job ("can you help me carry these out?").
- **Break up stuck groups** if a conversation has run out: "Sorry to steal you, I need you to meet someone."
- **Keep food and drinks topped up.** Better yet, a restocker friend does it (§6).
- **One highlight moment** gives the night a shape: the cake, a toast, a game, the s'mores, a group photo. Plan it about 60–70% of the way through.
- **Don't do dishes during the party.** Clear and stack them out of sight. Washing up tells guests it's over, which is only useful at the end (§12).
- **Eat and drink something yourself.** A host who is starving and stressed makes everyone tense.

## 12. Ending well

Ending well is as important as opening well, and most hosts never plan it.

**Plan your close:**
- **Last call** about 30 minutes before the end. Stop serving alcohol at least an hour before people leave; that's standard safety guidance.
- **Wind down the room:** music softer and slower, lights slightly up, coffee or tea and dessert out.
- **A closing moment:** "Thank you all for coming" with a last toast, a group photo, or a send-off for the guest of honor. It gives people a natural moment to leave.
- **Give a 10-minute heads-up** if it's a hard stop.
- **Leftovers to go** is a warm way to close. Have containers ready.
- **Stragglers:** be warm and direct: "I'm fading, I'm going to kick you all out. This was so fun." Or suggest a nearby bar if they want to keep going.
- **See people out** when you can, and check they've got a ride home.

## 13. Safety and care

- **Rides home:** ask about transportation plans before the party. Have a sober person or a ride-share plan. Collecting keys is an option at big drinking parties. Offer the couch.
- **Alcohol:** serve food along with drinks (protein, not just salty snacks). Don't top up glasses unasked. Make the NA option appealing. Never serve minors: the legal drinking age is **18 in Alberta, Manitoba and Quebec, and 19 in the rest of Canada**. Canadian courts have considered social-host liability, and the rules vary by province, so check yours. Your home insurance may not cover drunk-guest injuries. Cannabis follows the same care: provincial age rules, never pressure, never drive.
- **Allergies:** know who has a serious allergy and where their EpiPen is.
- **Accessibility:** stairs, seating for older guests or anyone with a mobility issue, a quiet room for anyone overwhelmed, step-free access if needed. Ask in the invite.
- **Emergency basics:** first aid kit, fire extinguisher, the address written down for calling 911. At a cottage, write down the **civic (911) address** and the nearest hospital; rural places are hard to find.
- **Outdoor / cottage:** provincial and township fire bans, water safety (no swimming alone or drunk at night), bug spray, sunscreen, flashlights, a weather plan B.
- **Consent and comfort:** take guests' "no" (to drinks, food, games, photos) without pushing. Some people don't want photos posted. Ask before posting.

---

## 14. After the party

- **That night:** food into the fridge, candles out, check the doors. Leave the rest for morning if you want.
- **Next day:** **a short thank-you** to everyone, and a special thanks to people who helped or brought things. Share photos (with permission).
- **Return dishes** people left behind (Hostbook tracks "whose dish is this").
- **Write a 3-line debrief for yourself** (Hostbook has a notes field for this):
  - What worked?
  - What would I change?
  - Who should I invite again / who clicked?
- Your next party will be better because of those notes.

## 15. Playbooks by party type

### Dinner party (4–10)
- Purpose sentence, then the guest list (chemistry matters most here).
- Menu: one make-ahead main, one easy starter, a bought or make-ahead dessert. Test new recipes beforehand, never for the first time on guests.
- Guests bring: wine or drinks, maybe a starter or dessert, never the main.
- Seat people on purpose (separate couples). One shared table question.
- Timeline: arrive 7:00 → snacks and drinks → sit 7:45 → main 8:15 → dessert 9:00 → coffee and close 10:30.

### Potluck (6–30)
- The host provides the main (or the theme's centerpiece), drinks, ice, and plates.
- Use the category sign-up (§6) with specific slots. Ask for dish labels and serving spoons.
- Leave space and heat: free fridge space, extension cord for slow cookers, an empty oven.
- Keep a "gaps" list for the final days (drinks, napkins, trash bags) and fill it yourself.

### Cocktail party / 2-hour party (10–40)
- Short and on a weeknight. Name tags. 2–3 icebreakers (start, middle, near the end). A firm end, said confidently.
- Simple drinks: one batch cocktail, one NA option, beer and wine. Finger food only.
- Timeline: 7:00 arrive and name tags → 7:20 first icebreaker → mingle → 8:15 second icebreaker → 8:50 last call → 9:00 thank you and close.

### Open house / drop-in
- A long window (e.g. 2–6 pm). Food that holds at room temperature. Restock in waves.
- Tell guests "come whenever, stay as long as you like."

### BBQ / backyard
- Grill master (host or assigned), meat and veg protein plus sides by sign-up.
- Shade, sunscreen, bug spray, coolers and lots of ice (double, it's hot). Trash and recycling bins visible.
- Rain plan B, decided ahead.
- Kids: a corner with lawn games and someone keeping an eye out.

### Game night (4–12)
- Choose games for the group size and **teach them quickly** (have the rules ready).
- Party games for big groups, strategy games for small. Have a short warm-up game for latecomers.
- Snacks that don't wreck cards: no greasy fingers, put out napkins.

### Movie night (3–12)
- **Seats first.** Everyone needs a view of the screen: count seats, then add floor cushions, beanbags and blankets. Dim the lights and kill the glare on the screen.
- **Pick before people arrive.** Shortlist 2–3 films that fit the group and let guests vote when they arrive (or in the group chat). Check the running time so you know the end.
- **Test the tech the day before:** streaming login, download it if the Wi-Fi is weak, sound, subtitles, and a backup film.
- **Quiet food.** Popcorn, candy, and finger food that doesn't crunch through the best scene. Serve it before the film starts, not halfway through.
- **Talk before and after, not during.** 30 minutes of chat and snacks first; a pause for refills if the film is over 2 hours; 15–30 minutes after the credits to talk about it. That's where the night's conversation happens.
- **Phones away** during the film (ask nicely, once).

### Cottage / weekend trip (4–16)
This one is many parties in a row. It needs roles.
- **Plan per meal:** each meal (Fri dinner, Sat breakfast, Sat lunch...) is assigned to a team of 2–3, who shop, cook, and clean up for it. Everyone cooks once, nobody cooks every time.
- **Shared groceries list** for staples (coffee, oil, butter, eggs, TP), split cost later.
- **Carpools:** who drives, who rides with whom, departure times, gas split.
- **Rooms and beds:** who sleeps where. Decide before arrival to avoid awkward negotiation.
- **Packing list for guests:** bedding/towels?, swimsuit, warm layer, rain jacket, slippers, games, bug spray.
- **Chores:** dishes rota, trash run, closing duties on the last day (fridge, bins, water, boats, locks).
- **One planned "big thing"** per day (hike, boat, sauna, games night) and lots of unplanned time.
- **Departure plan:** checkout time, who does the final sweep.

### Holiday party
- Invite 4–6 weeks out; December calendars fill fast.
- Expect people to come from other parties. An open-house window works well.
- A small, optional ritual (cookie exchange, ugly sweater, toast) gives it a center.

## 16. When things go wrong

| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| Nobody's here yet | That's why you seeded the room (§7). Otherwise: music up a bit, start something, don't apologize. |
| Room is too empty | Close off rooms, gather everyone in one area, move to the kitchen. People like being near each other. |
| Two groups won't mix | Introduce with hooks, run an icebreaker, start a game, or give mixed pairs a job. |
| Food is running out | Pull out freezer or pantry backups (always have one), order a pizza, it's fine. |
| Someone brought an uninvited +1 | Welcome them warmly at the party, clarify next time. |
| Someone's drunk | Water, food, a quiet spot, a sober friend, a safe ride home. Don't serve more. |
| A guest is alone all night | Give them a job, introduce them to a connector, check in later. |
| Spill / breakage | Laugh it off. Things matter less than people. |
| Weather ruins the outdoor plan | Your plan B was decided in advance (§15). |
| You're overwhelmed | Hand off a task. People like helping. |
| Bad conversation (politics, a fight) | Steer: "Okay, new topic, Sam, tell everyone about..." Take the heated person aside if needed. |

## 17. The host's mindset

- **Generous authority.** Parker's idea: guests *want* a host who takes charge, kindly. Introducing people, setting a few light rules, and ending on time are generous acts, not controlling ones.
- **Done beats perfect.** People remember how they felt, not whether the napkins matched.
- **Host for them, not for Instagram.** Take a few photos (people do want them), then put the phone down.
- **Host more often, smaller.** Practice makes you better and it gets easier. A small monthly dinner builds more friendship than one giant party a year.
- **Enjoy it.** Guests take their cue from you. If you're relaxed and having fun, they will too.
